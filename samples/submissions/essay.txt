Coastal Management and the Carbon Ledger

Flood defence schemes are still appraised almost entirely on avoided damage,
and the habitat consequences arrive late in the process as a constraint rather
than as a benefit. That framing made sense when the only quantifiable output
was property protection. It makes less sense now that sediment carbon can be
measured, because one of the options on the table produces both outcomes at
once and the appraisal cannot see the second one.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely. Vertical accretion keeps pace with
sea level rise wherever the suspended sediment supply is adequate, which means
the marsh surface climbs and the older organic layers pass below the
oxygenated zone where decomposition slows almost to a halt. So the store is a
function of geometry and sediment supply, not of how much vegetation is
visible at the surface in any given summer.

One review puts the consequence bluntly: "a marsh allowed to migrate landward
retains its carbon function, while one pinned against a seawall drowns and
releases it." The quoted framing is useful because it makes the decision a
choice between two engineering options rather than a trade-off between
engineering and nature.

Where I part company with the literature is on the appraisal timescale.
Accretion is slow and the discount rates used in flood appraisal are not, so a
sink that accumulates over eighty years contributes almost nothing to a
present value computed at three and a half percent. Arguing for realignment on
carbon grounds inside the existing appraisal framework is therefore close to
hopeless. The argument has to be made against the framework instead, which is
a harder but more honest position.

A second problem is measurement cost. Establishing an accretion rate at a
single site takes several years of marker horizon surveys, and schemes are
appraised in months. Any carbon benefit claimed at the appraisal stage is
therefore a modelled number, and modelled numbers in a business case tend to
harden into commitments.
