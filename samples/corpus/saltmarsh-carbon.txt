Burial, Not Growth: Why Saltmarsh Sediment Stores Carbon

Coastal saltmarsh sediment stores carbon at rates an order of magnitude above
those of temperate forest, and the mechanism is burial rather than growth.
Vertical accretion keeps pace with sea level rise wherever the suspended
sediment supply is adequate, which means the marsh surface climbs and the
older organic layers pass below the oxygenated zone where decomposition slows
almost to a halt. Above-ground biomass turns over every year and contributes
almost nothing to the long-term stock; the stock lives in the mud.

The practical consequence is that a marsh allowed to migrate landward retains
its carbon function, while one pinned against a seawall drowns and releases
it. Coastal squeeze is therefore a carbon problem as well as a habitat one,
and the timescale is short. Once the marsh platform falls below the level at
which vegetation can hold sediment, the accumulated organic layers are
reworked by tidal currents and re-exposed to oxygen.

Managed realignment buys two benefits at once, flood attenuation and a
persistent sink, and the second is rarely counted in the business case. The
omission is partly a measurement problem. Accretion rates vary by an order of
magnitude within a single estuary depending on distance from the sediment
source, elevation in the tidal frame, and the density of the vegetation
canopy, so a single site measurement extrapolated across a scheme is close to
worthless.

What the evidence does support is a ranking. Sites with an adequate sediment
supply and room to migrate are worth restoring first. Sites with neither
should be valued for flood attenuation alone, and any carbon claim attached
to them should be treated as speculative until several years of accretion
measurements exist.
